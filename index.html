An Yu Chen

Interactive Data Sphere: Gender, Scientific Knowledge, and Green Innovation

The work is relevant to researchers in innovation studies, gender and technology, science policy and economics, as well as policymakers, who are seeking to understand gender inequality in innovation systems.

Welcome to Data Sphere

This project is drawn from part of my PhD thesis, which investigates whether the gender composition of patent inventor teams influences the gender of the scientific researchers whose publications they cite. This study draws on open-access secondary data sources covering patent records, patent–science linkages, and scientific publications. PatentsView supplies U.S. patent records, disambiguated inventor identities, and inventor gender information. Inventor gender is inferred using the World Gender-Name Dictionary (WGND) 2.0, developed by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO). Green patents are classified according to the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) Y02/Y04S tagging scheme. To examine the scientific foundations of green technological innovation, the study uses the dataset developed by Marx and Fuegi (2020, 2022), which captures citations from U.S. patents to scientific publications. In addition, SciSciNet (Lin et al., 2023) provides publication-level and author-level information for analysing the gender composition of the scientific knowledge referenced in patents. Author gender is inferred through probabilistic, name-based attribution methods. The patent data cover the period from 1976 to 2020, together with linked information on patent citations to scientific publications.

The accompanying GitHub repository provides further information on the research design, Python-based data processing and analytical workflow underlying this project.

Originality and generality

Annual mean indicators, as supplied in the dataset.

Note: Originality captures the diversity of technological fields represented in backward citations, while generality reflects the technological breadth of forward citations. Both range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater technological diversity. Generality is not defined for patents without forward citations (Trajtenberg et al., 1997; Hall et al., 2001; Squicciarini et al., 2013).

Forward citations

Mean forward citations over five- and seven-year windows. Recent cohorts may have shorter observable citation histories depending on source construction.

Note: Forward citations accumulate over time. Recent patent cohorts have shorter citation observation windows and may therefore have fewer recorded citations. These measures may be affected by right-censoring and should be interpreted with caution.

How to read these estimates

Stage 1 models the probability that a patent is included in the known-gender science-citation sample (N = 400,219; pseudo-R² = 0.1861). Stage 2 models whether the patent has a positive female-authored science share among the selected science-using, known-gender observations (N = 83,352; pseudo-R² = 0.1805).

Important methodological qualification. Stage 2 includes an inverse Mills ratio (IMR) generated from Stage 1. The standard errors displayed here are the conventional Probit output supplied for that second-stage regression; they do not, by themselves, account for uncertainty from estimating the IMR. Treat inferential results as provisional until the selection model is re-estimated with an appropriate joint sample-selection estimator or a bootstrap that repeats both stages. A defensible exclusion restriction should also be justified.

Probit coefficients are on the latent-index scale and should not be read as percentage-point changes. Average marginal effects are easier to interpret as approximate changes in predicted probability for a one-unit change in a predictor, holding the model specification fixed. These are associations, not causal effects.
